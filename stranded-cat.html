Function First: Building Worlds That Work

Published on Aug 30, 2026

This is a local copy of the interview from Stranded Cat, conducted by Ibrahim Abdo.

From UX lead and startup founder to environment concept artist on Apex Legends, Dragon Age and Werewolf: The Apocalypse – Rageborn, Vladimir talks starting over in a room full of twenty-year-olds, why function has to come before beauty, and what stays human when a model can already fake the brushwork.

Vladimir Mokry spent his first career in UX design, co-founding startups and leading teams, before walking away in his early thirties to start over as a concept artist. He retrained at Syn Studio in Montreal and has since built environments and props for some of the biggest names in games: Apex Legends at Respawn, Dragon Age: The Veilguard at Volta, and now Werewolf: The Apocalypse – Rageborn at creā-ture Studios. An engineer by training and a teacher by instinct, he designs worlds that work before they try to impress. What follows is Vladimir, in his own words.

Career change

You were a UX lead and a co-founder before any of this. Then in your early thirties you started over as a concept artist. Why wasn't moving countries enough?

I burned out after my last startup. I spent a few months travelling around Asia, and I got close to taking a lead designer job at a fashion startup in Tokyo. Then one morning in December 2017, I had an epiphany. Tokyo would have been the same career with better weather. I needed to do more with my life than that.

From that morning, I knew it had to be art.

After that, the question was which kind of art. It had to pay the bills now, not be the weekend thing, and I had no idea which kind that would be. Illustration first, maybe fine art. Growing up in Eastern Europe, it had never occurred to me that “concept artist” was a job somebody had. Games and films just existed, the way weather exists. Once I found out what the work actually was, that settled it. You build something out of nothing, using every reference and every bit of culture you've absorbed, and then a few million people look at it or play it.

Then it became a budgeting problem. I looked at a school in Singapore and at ArtCenter in Pasadena. Both excellent, both far out of reach. The one I found in Montreal looked slightly shady to me at first. It was only in its third year of running a concept art program. But it was the one I could theoretically afford if I got a loan from friends. I knew I needed the structure, because I felt that if I tried to learn everything by myself, laziness would win.

I didn't tell many people. The ones who found out split about evenly. Some said I was too old, that I had a good business, that I was an expert in my field (UX), and that I should buy a flat and start a family. Others said that we all have just one life and that it's worth fighting for. Even if I failed, at least I would have no regrets.

Moving to Canada was the easy part. The hard part was agreeing to be bad at something again in a room full of twenty-year-olds.

Starting over

You arrived at Syn Studio in Montreal after years of being the senior person in the room. What surprised you, and what did you have to unlearn?

I was the oldest one there, and I'd gotten in off a waiting list. Nobody else in the class knew their position on that list. I knew mine. It was, well, the worst one. So I walked in assuming I was the worst artist in the room. That's uncomfortable in a specific way.

I expected to be bad at drawing. What I didn't expect was that my old career refused to stay in the past. I had years of UX and leadership experience, and a degree in pedagogy, and I couldn't switch the lens off. I started seeing problems in how the school taught. Some instructors were excellent artists and mediocre teachers. Some were the other way around. I don't think there's a shortcut around it. You need to be both. I said so, repeatedly, and loudly enough that the school still isn't thrilled with me. My delivery was probably part of that.

The funny part is that although the school leadership cannot forgive me, my school-related YouTube videos drove them tens of fee-paying students.

The entrepreneur in me didn't sit still either. I started designing a feedback tool for students on the side and pitched it around Montreal's startup scene. Now I see that this was a mistake. The tool was a decent idea, but I had 18 months to draw as much as humanly possible, and I spent part of them quietly building a startup again.

You asked what I had to unlearn. I don't have a clean answer for that. It was subtler than one wrong assumption. I never got to just be a student. I was half student and half educator the whole time, grading the class against my own standards for teaching. Good for spotting what was broken. Less good for being someone people wanted to sit next to.

People always ask whether starting later is an advantage. What I actually had was no floor. A lot of my classmates had parents funding the year, so if it didn't work out for them, they had a house to go back to. I had nothing under me. It kept me drawing through the night.

Art meets production

When does a beautiful concept become a design that actually works in a game?

I believe the goal of concept art is to deliver function first.

That instinct is older than art school for me. I studied engineering in high school in Czechia. Pillars, bridges, how a differential works, the difference between two-stroke and four-stroke engines. Which is funny in retrospect, because engineering was itself a retreat from art. At fourteen, I didn't believe I had what it took to continue art studies, so I took the safe technical route.

It took another seventeen years, four startups and a whole career in UX to get back.

That engineering knowledge never went anywhere. It shows up in how a production line gets laid out in a factory, or how you set up the layout of an upper-class flat in a cyberpunk game. Somebody still has to make those calls.

I also don't like being siloed. Handing over a pretty image and walking away has never interested me. When I give feedback to another artist – 2D, 3D or level design – I try to lead with the reason. “This bridge falls down without proper pillars.” “This part has to connect to the engine, the wheels need proper suspension, etc.” It's how an engineer, architect or urban planner thinks. That logic underneath is what makes a world believable, much more than the rendering does.

An example. On Apex Legends, I designed a new traversal system for the map called ZipRail. A next-generation zipline, essentially. I went full research mode: electromagnetic suspension and superconductive magnets. That is fun! I spent time exploring more complicated, realistic and grounded designs that had multiple shock absorbers, but in the end, for simplicity and polygon budget, the simpler design won.

The part I'm still annoyed about came later. I built a solid 3D model for it. Our outsourcing supplier never opened the file. They rebuilt it from scratch off the concepts (and read them wrong!) with square-based columns instead of my triangle-based ones, which follow the design language in the Apex Legends art bible, and it devalued my work.

When I complained, it was too late, as we had no schedule left to fix it. I have no control over anyone downstream of me. Now I over-communicate, clarify details, check at every handoff, and assume the file can come back as something else.

Believable worlds

You'd rather fly somewhere and stand in a street than build a reference board. What do you end up noticing that never makes it onto a board?

What I like about Japan is how building code and zoning enforce local architecture. For example, there is a regulation limiting how much shadow a new building can throw onto its neighbours. Or noise-cumulative zoning laws. With constraints like these, you sometimes get extraordinary buildings that would not work elsewhere.

Then there are the outskirts of Tokyo, the parts nobody photographs for social networks. I love walking around random neighbourhoods and finding how people solve very small amounts of space. How somebody has arranged their plant pots on a tiny ledge. How a narrow alley still manages to hold a bike rack, two vending machines and a bench. None of that is on a reference board. You have to be standing in it.

Prague and Central Europe taught me something else, which is density done right. A lot of neighbourhoods still hold to five or six floors and no higher. That turns out to be close to the right number of people per square kilometre: enough to keep several cafes alive within a short walk, not so many that you are constantly bumping into strangers. I am aware that I am biased by my European background, though.

All of it ends up in the work. And because it comes out of my own walking around and B-roll footage that I take for my YouTube channel, it doesn't look like anyone else's reference.

Dragon Age: The Veilguard

Dragon Age comes with years of visual language and a lot of people with opinions about it. How much freedom do you actually get?

In some areas, enough. In others, not as much. I worked on Dragon Age: The Veilguard early in my career, when I was at an outsource studio called Volta. This was the first “huge” project where you immediately felt the weight.

Working as an outsource partner means that everything has to go through several rounds of approval. Volta already had a close relationship with BioWare even before I joined them. What changed during my time on the project was more personal. As I delivered concepts faster and more in line with the Art Direction guidelines, they (BioWare) started to trust me more and more, and sometimes I was able to talk directly to people at BioWare to save time that could possibly be lost in translation.

But the freedom has its limits, and some tasks show that clearly. There was a map of Thedas I worked on. I love making maps. I once designed a map of Prague that was printed and reprinted to a total of 170,000 copies for travellers who landed at Prague Airport. The brief changed under my hands. I reworked and reworked it, but eventually the task went to another artist. That stung. I wish I had pushed the mood and contrast harder at the start, or fought for a better brief.

But that is the lifecycle of the concept. The concept, at the end, is just a concept. It gets overpainted, thrown out, put to the backlog or turned into something else. Most of what I designed for Dragon Age never made it into the final game. Everyone in this industry makes peace with it.

Apex Legends

Apex Legends never really finishes, there's another season every few months. How did that change the way you design? And what did you hide in E-District that nobody notices?

The biggest thing Apex and Respawn taught me is that the world is never finished. Every three months, a season ships whether you are happy with your art or not. Some are small, a handful of balance changes and cosmetics. Some are a real content drop. Either way, the clock never stops. Done is better than perfect. That limitation forces real discipline about how much time you should spend on each task.

The easter eggs: yes, most of what's hidden in E-District is mine. Beyond the street signs with our team's names on them, I used spare time to design posters and a couple of funny pinball machines for the arcade. The best use of downtime I've had in this industry.

The details I'm proudest of are hidden in the drop zone most players don't go to, the Dam. I went down a long rabbit hole researching how real dams operate. Gameplay needed the water reservoir kept low (otherwise players would think that they could try to land on the water surface), so the spillway intakes on the back side are exposed rather than submerged. The brief was to have a cool water slide as one of the features. Well, how do you want to run water from the top when drought conditions keep the level low? Eventually, I routed the water back up through reverse pumps. Another detail that I designed is a full-size all-axis crane wedged between two towers, specifically positioned so that it could move parts to the top service door of every turbine. That is storytelling. I like designing that.

Werewolf: The Apocalypse – Rageborn

World of Darkness is a very different register from Apex or Dragon Age. What artistic challenges does it bring?

The artistic challenge is the style itself. We are working with the 5th edition of WoD, and our goal is to match, at least to a certain level, the visual language that was already established in the books: a less realistic look, with visible brushwork. That is easy to hold in a painting and hard to hold in Unreal 5. The engine wants physically based materials and real-time lighting, and both of those pull everything back towards photoreal. Keeping the hand visible through that pipeline is a challenge for our technical artists as much as for me.

Beyond that, though, the bigger thing I have learned here is operational: what it's actually like to work in a small team.

I'm trying to be a generalist, but I joined as the second concept artist, so my lane became environments and props. I enjoy designing characters and creatures as much as props and environments, but as my colleague Arman handles them, I focus on building the backstage, literally and figuratively, so the protagonists and antagonists have a place to play their part. Anything with space, mood or atmosphere eventually finds its way onto the canvas in Photoshop.

Being part of the small team also means that I can offer my help earlier in the design process. Sometimes I find myself at the start of blocking out an area, sharing ideas about gameplay and design implications even before there is a visual concept for it. My overlap with other disciplines is much wider here than it was at Respawn. I can't say much more than that yet. Only that I enjoy it a lot.

White Cliffs

White Cliffs looks like a break from your professional work. What were you chasing?

A limited palette, mostly. It started as a sketch of a ship levitating past a tall wall, coming in to dock at a short bridge that juts out of it, both casting long shadows on the surfaces. The final piece drifted a little bit from that sketch, but that is okay.

I explored various textures for the wall, and the curly, organic one won. It also allowed me to find the limitations of texture tessellation in Blender. Once I liked the main shapes of this alien world, I invented a backstory: canyons carved by rivers of cupric chloride, and vessels harvesting mineral cores with explosives.

In the end, I also included some concept art tropes, such as the classic lone figure with a stick leading the viewer towards the focal point. And when the piece gave me an opportunity for a blue-red contrast, I committed to it. Yes, I was inspired by the concept artist Pascal Blanché here, at least slightly.

On AI and the craft

You work in a field plenty of people have already written off because of AI. What part of the job will it actually take, and what part won't?

My threshold for panic is high. I do use AI for grammar checks. English is not my first language, so in professional conversations I sometimes do a simple check to sound better. In personal conversations with my loved ones, on the other hand, I don't use AI at all. Writing with mistakes is a reminder for them that they are still talking to a human.

What AI changed is reference gathering, but not for the better. A huge portion of what comes back when you search for photo references is AI-generated now. Most of us can't tell the difference anymore. The whole method of reference search was about getting results that were grounded in something that actually existed. But now the well is spoiled. I regularly have to set date limits to before 2020-ish in search engines to get back what was lost.

As for what stays human in the job, this connects back to the point I made earlier. A model can fake beauty and brushwork very well. What it can't give you (yet) is the reason a shape exists.

Am I anxious? Sure, the same as anyone who works with computers these days. But if a model ever gets to the point where it can design and structurally calculate a whole building on its own, how to space the load-bearing columns, how sunlight will be distributed through the rooms, how to run the water pipes and the electrical routing, all of it, then concept art being automated is the last thing humans should be worried about.

Closing thoughts

What did you believe starting out that turned out to be completely wrong?

Summer of 2018, right after the loan from friends came through, I had a moment of doubt. I went back to an old dream I'd never told anyone about: becoming a commercial pilot. Then I did some reasoning. I asked myself which career direction was more vulnerable to automation and AI.

My answer at the time was that pilots were finished. The sky was empty airspace. Navigation was a solved problem. Thanks to the Instrument Landing System, planes already landed themselves in bad weather. Obviously, pilots would lose their jobs.

I was spectacularly wrong, and it took me some years to understand why.

The answer is simple: aviation has a body count. Because of that, the industry built an enormous moat around itself: certification, decades of regulation, safety frameworks, the cost of replacing entire fleets with something autonomous. We, artists, never killed anyone. Which turned out to be terrible for our job security. Nobody builds a moat around a job where the worst case is an ugly poster.

So the job I thought was about to be replaced turned out to be one of the most protected professions there is.

The one I chose has no protection at all.

I think about that more often than I'd like to.
